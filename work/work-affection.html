SC. 02

WHAT I DID

I mixed this from the artist's initial multitrack in Logic Pro with three main mix passes. Initially, a static mix and general cleanup — getting general levels and checking for any recording issues or performance mistakes so they were fixed. Then the main creative mix, which included the bulk of the work: everything from general EQ and compression on single tracks and buses to creative decisions such as the intro — this was where the majority of the mixing took place. Finally, a last pass to check nothing was overlooked and the target loudness was reached. The mix aimed for a relaxed and natural sound: vocals and guitar solo at the front, the guitar holding the main body, and the drums sitting behind everything as the backbone. Below: problems I faced and specific creative decisions I made, each with an explanation and screenshots.

Reference tracks

Four references, each picked for a target

I selected reference tracks using the same method I would apply for a client — following Dom Morley's mix-management approach in Sound on Sound: decide the specific roles or targets each reference should serve, then find tracks to meet those aims, rather than choosing songs by feel.

  • —Home — Cavetown. Genre/feel target; vocal doubling, harmony blending, subtle atmospheric reverbs.
  • —The Wrong Direction — Passenger. Acoustic-guitar clarity and tone that never overpowers the vocal.
  • —That Distant Shore — Steven Universe. Role model for the background crystal synth and the outro.
  • —Be Easy — Toby Bain. Artist reference — used to understand the client's preferred sound and mix outcome.

Gain staging

Cleaning and levelling the session first

First pass cleaned and levelled everything before any creative processing — because creative plugins only work on a balanced mix.

  • —Tracks organised, coloured and parked into folders and auxes; no template, auxes built for this song only.
  • —Resonance hunting and notch-filtering pass across every track in isolation, then instrument groups, then the full mix.
  • —High-pass on kick at 12 kHz, steep low-pass on vocal at 100 Hz, 12 dB of muddiness removed.
  • —Gain staged to −12 → −9 dB peaks on the first pass (headroom for plugins), then remixed to −6 dB peak for mastering.

Bass

Re-amping the bass, virtually

Most muddy track in the session. The bass amp was fighting the kick and sub-kick for 50–200 Hz whilst lacking articulation. Since it was a DI bass recording I was able to virtually reamp it using Logic's native Bass Amp Designer, using a Neumann U 87-style microphone emulation alongside the amp's graphic EQ to reduce the muddiness and improve the clarity. Proper hardware re-amping wasn't possible within the assignment's time and resources, so this was done in the box. This was then followed by a mix of static and dynamic EQ and multiband compression to fine-tune the tone of the bass.

  • —Amp-EQ + graphic-EQ, driven like a real re-amp (shown above).
  • —Modelled Neumann U87 placed between the bass and mid drivers — its 9 kHz bump returns the articulation.
  • —Dynamic + static cut of the masked 50–100 Hz region (Pro-Q 3, shown above).
  • —Multiband compressor keyed off the toms (Pro-MB, shown above) — cutting only the fundamental range so bass stays present between tom hits.
  • —iZotope Guitar De-noise cleared playing squeaks.

Guitar solo

Smoothness for the guitar solo

The solo replaces the vocal at the centre of the song after chorus one — it has to be as sonically smooth as the voice it stands in for.

  • —Resonance notches, then Logic Amp Designer as a transparent character-pass — studio amp, modern cabinet, small gain boost, slight mid cut for articulation.
  • —Modelled Royer R-121 ribbon, off-axis and pulled back from the cone so the high-end could breathe.
  • —FabFilter Saturn 2 — very light tilt-EQ saturation (low cut, high boost) for warmth, never harshness.
  • —Multiband compressor keyed from the crystal-synth "noise" track, active only in the hi-mids where articulation lives — 2 dB above playing level, fast attack, medium ratio, letting the crystal synths poke through while the guitar keeps its body.

Tape samples

Bedding in the tape samples

Hard-coded tape samples stick out like a sore thumb. Two moves made them feel in-place instead of pasted-on.

  • —A "dustbin" reverb at huge size — pushing so much of the direct signal back that the sample's interference-like artefacts softened into the background.
  • —DearVR Micro binaural panner automated front-to-back and left-to-right — the sample drifts around the stereo field as the track plays, adding both depth and motion.

Bus treatment

Three aux buses and a mixbus

Kept the aux count deliberately small — three buses plus a mixbus — to preserve the natural feel of the song.

  • —Vocal plate: separate from the instrument plate; no predelay, shorter tail, 90 Hz low cut — reverb felt rather than heard.
  • —Instrument plate: 60 ms predelay so transients and reverb each get their own space; low cut and low shelf removing reverb mud (Abbey-road style).
  • —Quantec room bus: parallel to the mixbus, returning lost room tone to the isolation-recorded tracks.
  • —Mixbus: light glue compression — −8 dB threshold, 1.9:1 ratio, 3 dB wet boost — and a 1.6 dB air-boost at 11 kHz that only reveals itself on A/B.

Production moments

Three moments, built by editing alone

Intro

I wanted the intro to ease naturally into the song while staying true to the direction of the original multitrack. Reversed guitar with stereo spread focused on the mids and high-mids leads into the original chord through a dense EMT reverb, then a quieter reversed clip tapers away — a gradual transition into the track that doesn't feel out of place.

Guitar solo build

The transition into the solo already worked, but I felt it could hit harder with a little editing. Stripped the drums back to kick and snare, raising the snare around 8 dB to give the fill more weight and nudging its timing, which became noticeable once the other drums were gone. Bringing in the crash on the following downbeat completes the transition and draws attention to the solo.

Outro

Taking the crystal-like plucks in "That Distant Shore" as inspiration, the synth ends the song calmly and lingeringly. The mix cross-fades into a version of the crystal synth raised by around 6 dB, and the final note pans left to right with each delay repeat — the movement gives the ending a little more life while the track winds down gently.

SC. 03

PROCESS NOTES

Notes behind the decisions, written for this portfolio — how the mix was aimed, checked and put together.

Overall aim

The song asked for a relaxed atmosphere — closer in feel to cool jazz / hard bop with instrument roles shuffled than to a standard band mix. Vocals and guitar solo lead; the acoustic guitar holds its own space; drums sit back in loudness. Everything from reference choice to bus design was judged against that one aim.

On working with the artist

Reference choice included a discussion-with-the-client step: a Sound on Sound article on mix management prompted checking the artist's influences directly, and also accepting that a previous release "doesn't mean you know what they are looking for this time. Artists change and evolve." Where a creative decision rested on taste (a drastic bass treatment, the Quantec room bus) it is flagged as a point to bring back to the artist — the mix must serve their vision.

Verification

Critical listening in stereo and mono, at normal, louder and quieter levels, on both calibrated headphones (Sennheiser HD 206 + Sonarworks SoundID Reference) and KRK Classic 5 monitors — before signing off any pass.

Workflow, in three passes

  1. Cleanup: listen through, organise folders/auxes, rough mix, then remove resonances and cut unneeded high/low content on every track.
  2. Creative: compression and reverb sends — staying natural, never more plugin than the song asks for.
  3. Final remix: gentle re-balance to −6 dB peak, room for mastering, then full critical-listening sign-off.
